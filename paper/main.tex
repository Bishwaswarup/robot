\title[Gait-phase-aware command timing for quadruped swarms]{Gait-Aware Command Timing for Decentralized Quadruped Swarms}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Bishwaswarup} \sur{Nayak}\,\orcidlink{0009-0001-9926-5329}}\email{bishwaswarup@iisc.ac.in}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Physics}, \orgname{Indian Institute of Science}, \orgaddress{\city{Bengaluru}, \postcode{560012}, \state{Karnataka}, \country{India}}}

\abstract{Multi-robot collision avoidance treats each robot as a point that can change velocity at any
instant. A trotting quadruped cannot: how well it absorbs a change of commanded velocity depends on where
in the gait cycle the change arrives. We measure this dependence for a B\'ezier trot controller on a
simulated Unitree Go1 and build a decentralized swarm layer around it. Each robot solves reciprocal
collision avoidance (ORCA) one gait period ahead, holds each non-urgent velocity change for at most half a
gait period, releases it at the phase a capability map predicts is least destabilizing, and shapes it with
a gait-aware slew limiter; urgent conflicts bypass the hold. In MuJoCo, in crowded or obstructed scenarios
with 6, 9, 16 and 32 robots, the full system reduced urgent avoidance events by 22--39\% and 99th-percentile body
tilt by 19--24\% relative to a reactive baseline (paired by seed, Holm-corrected). It had no falls in 230
runs, against two in 170 baseline runs, and fewer robot contacts, though not none. A control that releases after a random wait of the same length
shows that prediction and the bounded hold produce the reduction in urgent events, while the phase choice
adds a further 4--6\% reduction in tilt. Replaying 488 recorded swarm events at nine release times shows
that the map's phase ranking, accurate for isolated steps, transfers only partly to the rapid command
sequences of a swarm. With four robots the method increases urgent events; with eight it does not
significantly change them.}

\keywords{Legged robots, Multi-robot systems, Collision avoidance, Gait phase, Quadruped swarms, ORCA}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Decentralized collision avoidance methods such as reciprocal velocity obstacles and ORCA
\cite{vandenberg2008rvo,vandenberg2011orca} give each robot a new velocity every control tick. They assume
that the platform can follow that velocity, possibly subject to an acceleration limit. Wheeled robots and
multirotors come close to that assumption. A trotting quadruped does not: its body is supported by two
diagonal feet at a time, the support line rotates during each stance, and a velocity change that arrives
at the wrong moment produces a large body tilt or a stumble.

That a legged gait responds to a disturbance differently at different phases is well known in
single-robot locomotion: push recovery, capture-point and step-timing methods all exploit it
\cite{pratt2006capture,khadiv2016step,chen2022capturability,hobbelen2007gsn}. What has not been studied,
to our knowledge, is the consequence for multi-robot navigation. A swarm layer that issues frequent
avoidance commands is a source of disturbances to every robot's gait. Formation and swarm controllers for
quadrupeds \cite{zhan2025safe,noren2026role} shape \emph{what} each robot is commanded, but not \emph{when}
within the gait the command is delivered.

This paper asks whether timing the swarm layer's velocity changes to the gait phase makes a quadruped
swarm safer, and at what cost, and separates two mechanisms that such timing combines: holding a
non-urgent change for a short, bounded time, and choosing the gait phase at which it is released. We
answer it in simulation with a fully open stack and paired statistics. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A measurement of phase-dependent fragility for a B\'ezier trot on a Unitree Go1 model: a pilot
    experiment, a capability map over phase, direction and size of a command change (held-out
    $R^2$ 0.94--0.98 for isolated steps, against 0.70--0.87 without phase), and a push-impulse envelope in
    which knowing the phase raises the tolerable impulse by a factor of 1.33--1.44.
  \item A decentralized command layer for quadruped swarms: ORCA solved one gait period ahead, a
    commander that holds each non-urgent change for at most half a gait period and releases it at the
    phase the map predicts is safest, a gait-aware slew limiter, and an urgent bypass.
  \item An ablation and scaling study over 4--32 robots, a gate, pillar fields and a 53\,m obstacle course
    (1{,}110 runs, paired by seed, Holm-corrected), with controls that release after a random wait of the
    same length or at the map's worst phase. Prediction and the bounded hold produce the reduction in
    urgent events; the phase choice adds a smaller, consistent reduction in body tilt.
  \item A test of the capability map in the context where it is used: 488 recorded swarm events replayed at
    nine release times. The map's phase ranking transfers only partly from isolated steps to the rapid
    command sequences of a swarm, and we identify where it holds.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Reciprocal collision avoidance}
Velocity obstacles and their reciprocal forms
\cite{vandenberg2008rvo,vandenberg2011orca} are the standard decentralized method for crowds of robots;
ORCA reduces each control tick to a small linear program. Extensions add platform limits:
acceleration-velocity obstacles bound the acceleration \cite{vandenberg2011avo}, and NH-ORCA bounds the
tracking error of non-holonomic robots \cite{alonsomora2013nhorca}. All of these model the platform's
limits as constant in time. We keep ORCA unchanged and instead model a limit that varies periodically with
the gait.

\subsection{Multi-quadruped formation and navigation}
Recent work on quadruped teams uses distributed model
predictive control with control barrier functions \cite{zhan2025safe} or leader--follower formations with
path planning \cite{noren2026role}. These treat each quadruped as a velocity-controlled point and rely on
the locomotion controller to follow the commands. The phase-aware layer proposed here is complementary: it
can sit between any such planner and the locomotion controller.

\subsection{Phase-dependent response of legged gaits}
The capture point \cite{pratt2006capture}, step-timing
adaptation \cite{khadiv2016step} and switched-system capturability for quadrupeds
\cite{chen2022capturability} all show that what a legged robot can recover from depends on its state
within the step, and gait-sensitivity analysis \cite{hobbelen2007gsn} measures how disturbances propagate
through a limit cycle. These results are used to choose footholds and step times for one robot. We use the
same fact one level up: to choose \emph{when} the navigation layer changes its command.

\subsection{Command shaping for legged robots}
Velocity commands to legged controllers are commonly rate-limited, and learned controllers are trained on
curricula of command changes. A recent example schedules commands by the robot's current velocity to
extend its top speed \cite{tao2026speed}; the aim there is the locomotion envelope of one robot. Our
question differs in purpose: we choose \emph{when} a navigation-layer velocity change, issued for
collision avoidance in a team, reaches the locomotion controller. To the best of our knowledge, existing multi-robot navigation approaches do not explicitly schedule navigation-layer command release according to the gait phase of the receiving legged robot..

\section{The low-level controller and its phase-dependent fragility}\label{sec:controller}

\subsection{Controller}
Each robot runs the same open-source trot controller. The feet follow a 12-point
B\'ezier swing curve \cite{hyun2014cheetah,rahme2020spotmini} in a diagonal trot (period 0.36\,s, duty factor
0.5, swing clearance 0.07\,m, hip-to-foot height 0.27\,m). A PI loop on body velocity ($k_i = 1.5$) removes
steady drift, Raibert foot placement \cite{raibert1986legged} (gain 0.1) corrects velocity errors, and joint
targets go to the position actuators of the MuJoCo Menagerie Go1 model
\cite{todorov2012mujoco,zakka2022menagerie}. A slew limiter bounds the rate of change of the command at
$(1.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}, 1.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}, 3.0\,\mathrm{rad/s^2})$ for $(v_x, v_y, \omega_z)$. The
controller tracks commands to within about 0.02\,m/s, survives 4\,min of random command changes without a
fall, and walks at heights from 0.16\,m to 0.34\,m.

\subsection{Stability}
Treating one gait period as a stroboscopic Poincar\'e map, the largest Floquet
multiplier is 0.42--0.59 for the full controller and 0.73--0.87 open loop, so the gait is orbitally stable
over the commanded speed range. Stability of the cycle does not say how a single command change is
absorbed, which depends on when it arrives.

\subsection{Why the phase matters}
In a trot the body is supported by one diagonal pair at a time: front-right
with rear-left, then front-left with rear-right, at roughly $-34^\circ$ and $+34^\circ$ to the heading for the
Go1's geometry. Within one stance the effective support axis rotates from about $+25^\circ$ to $+65^\circ$ as
the body moves over the feet. A change of commanded velocity that pushes the body across the current support
axis is resisted by the stance legs; one that pushes along it tips the body about that axis. The same change
therefore produces different tilt at different phases, and by the mirror symmetry of the trot the pattern
repeats every half period with the lateral direction flipped.

\subsection{Measured fragility}
A pilot experiment applied command steps and body pushes at controlled phases.
Phase had a large effect: in 27 of 64 push conditions, whether the robot recovered depended on the phase
alone. From these data we built a capability map: a lookup table, periodic in phase and symmetrized, that
predicts peak tilt and fall risk for a command change of a given direction and size at a given phase
(Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}a). On held-out trials it explains 94--98\% of the variance, against 70--87\% for the
same model without phase. A push-impulse envelope found by bisection (Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}b) agrees with the
pilot in 98.6\% of cases, and knowing the phase raises the impulse the robot tolerates by a factor of
1.33--1.44.

\subsection{What this means for a swarm}
In isolated step tests, releasing a command at the best phase
strictly dominated the slew limiter alone. In a stream of swarm commands, however, timing alone did not work:
only 0--2\% of the commands resembled the settled single steps the map was measured on, and about half were
urgent, leaving no time to wait for a good phase. Section~\ref{sec:method} is designed around that finding:
the swarm layer must look ahead so that most changes are known before they are needed.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

Every robot runs the same four-stage stack at 10\,Hz (Fig.~\ref{fig:stack}), with no central planner and no
communication beyond the neighbour positions and velocities that ORCA needs. A safety layer that is
identical in every configuration, the baseline included, wraps the stack.

\subsection{Predictive swarm layer}
Each robot is a disc of radius 0.38\,m with maximum speed 0.6\,m/s.
Rather than solving ORCA for the present, the robot solves it for the situation one lookahead $L$ ahead
($L = 0.36$\,s, one gait period). Its own position is extrapolated with the command it is currently executing,
and its neighbours with their measured velocities. The result is the velocity the robot will need at time
$t + L$, so it can be requested with a deadline of $L$. If the current command would collide within 0.8\,s
with a robot, or within 1.5\,s with a static obstacle, the request is urgent: ORCA is solved for the present
and the command is released at once. A $15^\circ$ keep-right bias breaks symmetric deadlocks.
Each robot sees the positions and velocities of up to ten neighbours within 4\,m, without noise or delay;
velocities are estimated from positions with a 0.15\,s low-pass filter.

\subsection{Phase-aware commander}
A request whose change from the current command exceeds 0.15\,m/s (or 0.3\,rad/s) is a manoeuvre. A
non-urgent manoeuvre is held for at most half a gait period (0.18\,s), which is always shorter than its
deadline $L$. Over that window the commander queries the capability map at 1/64-period steps and releases
the command at the phase with the lowest predicted cost, namely peak tilt plus a weight of 50 on the
predicted fall probability. Smaller changes, urgent requests and changes outside the range of the map's
grid of speeds, directions and sizes are released at once. Every other manoeuvre is timed, including the
many that occur during walking unlike the settled, straight steps on which the map was measured
(Section~\ref{sec:map-context} tests the map on exactly these). A new request similar to a pending one
updates the pending command but keeps its release time, so the timing is not reset every tick.

\subsection{Gait-aware slew limiter}
Once released, a change is applied through a slew limiter whose rate
depends on phase. The limiter is built from the capability map: it ramps faster in phases where the map
predicts little tilt for the current direction of change and slower where it predicts a lot. Its rate is
scaled so that its mean over one period equals that of the uniform limiter, so it does not gain speed by
being more aggressive on average.

\subsection{Formation and queueing}
Formations follow a reference point with slot offsets that shrink
laterally when obstacles are close ahead. In bottlenecks, the robot further back takes 90\% of the avoidance
responsibility in ORCA and the robot in front 10\%, so that robots queue instead of shouldering through.

\subsection{Shared safety layer}
In every configuration, ORCA sees static obstacles inflated by 0.1\,m with a
2\,s horizon. Body commands are clipped to the range in which the gait was stress-tested
($|\omega_z| \le 1.0$\,rad/s, $|v_y| \le 0.3$\,m/s), and forward speed is scaled from 100\% to 30\% as the
heading error grows from $30^\circ$ to $90^\circ$, so a robot turns before it walks. If ORCA returns less than
0.05\,m/s for more than 1\,s while the robot wants to move, the preferred velocity is turned $75^\circ$
towards the formation reference for 2\,s, and to the other side if the robot is still stuck. Each of these
was added after a failure found in the experiments; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} reports one further fix that
we tried and rejected.

\subsection{Scope of the safety layer}\label{sec:safety-scope}
ORCA returns velocities that are collision-free for ideal agents that adopt them at once and keep them.
A quadruped does neither, and the stack handles the gap heuristically rather than with a guarantee.
(i) Future positions are predicted by constant-velocity extrapolation over $L$: the robot's own position
with the command it is executing, its neighbours' with their measured velocities. (ii) The selected
velocity becomes the active command after the heading controller and the body-command limits (Section~\ref{sec:method}),
when the commander releases it, at most 0.18\,s after the request; the slew limiter then ramps the gait
towards it. (iii) The prediction does not model the robot's own tracking error: the lag of the ramp and
the controller's tracking error enter only through the neighbours' measured velocities and through
replanning every 0.1\,s. (iv) A release cannot miss its deadline, because the longest hold (0.18\,s) is
shorter than $L$; the ramp after release can, however, extend past $t+L$. (v) A request is urgent, and
bypasses both prediction and the hold, when the time to collision of the current command is below 0.8\,s
with a robot or 1.5\,s with a static obstacle. (vi) The safety layer therefore reduces collision risk but
does not ensure it: the full system had 16 robot--robot and 1 robot--obstacle contact onsets in 230 runs
(Section~\ref{sec:safety-totals}).
The collision avoidance reported here is validated empirically, not proven.

\cite{zakka2022menagerie} in one scene (foot friction 0.9). The swarm layer runs at 10\,Hz and the gait
controller at the physics rate. Each seed perturbs every start position by 5\,cm (standard deviation) and
gives every robot a random initial gait phase, so robots are never phase-locked. Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}
lists the scenarios and Table~\ref{tab:configs} the configurations.

\subsection{Map validation in context}\label{sec:replay-setup}
To test the capability map where it is used, we run the full system on the 8- and 16-robot swaps, the
9-robot pillar field and the gate (5 seeds each) and sample one in four of the manoeuvres the commander
times, at most 30 per run (488 events). At each sampled request the complete simulation state is saved.
From it, the same change is released after each of nine waits from 0 to half a gait period, while every
robot's logged command stream continues as recorded, and the peak tilt of the focal robot over the next
1.0\,s is measured. Replaying the commander's own choice reproduces the original run exactly in every
event. For each event this gives a measured tilt at every candidate phase, so the map can be scored on
absolute error, on how well it ranks the phases, and on the tilt of the phase it would choose.

\subsection{Metrics}
Three metrics are fixed in advance as primary: urgent avoidance events per robot-minute
(how often a robot had to change course with less than 0.8\,s to spare), the 99th-percentile body tilt over
the run, and the closest robot--robot distance. We also report falls (trunk below 0.15\,m or tilted more than
$60^\circ$), runs not finished within the time limit, contacts and completion time.

\subsection{Statistics}
Comparisons are paired by seed. For each metric we take the per-seed difference and test it with an exact
two-sided sign-flip permutation test that enumerates all $2^n$ sign patterns ($n$ = number of seeds);
zero differences are kept and do not change the result, and ties with the observed statistic count as
extreme. We also report 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals. Three families of tests are corrected
separately with the Holm method. (A) The 21 primary tests compare the full system with the reactive
baseline (7 scenarios $\times$ 3 metrics). (B) Twelve tests compare prediction with timing against
prediction alone on the four main scenarios. (C) The timing controls compare the full system with its
random-wait and worst-phase versions. With $n = 10$ seeds the smallest possible raw $p$-value is
$2/2^{10} \approx 0.00195$; Holm multiplies the $k$-th smallest of the 21 primary $p$-values by $22-k$, so
a 10-seed test ranked first could at best reach $21 \times 0.00195 \approx 0.041$, below 0.05 but never
below 0.01. Holm is a step-down procedure: the adjusted value of the $k$-th test is the largest of
$(22-j)\,p_{(j)}$ over $j \le k$, capped at 1. In our data nine of the twelve 20-seed tests have smaller
$p$-values than any 10-seed test, so the six significant 10-seed tests, tied at $p = 2/2^{10}$, occupy ranks
10 to 15 and all receive the adjusted value $12 \times 0.00195 \approx 0.023$ of the first of them.
Table~\ref{tab:holm} in the appendix lists all 21 raw and adjusted $p$-values in order. The scaling suite
can therefore reach the 5\% level but not the 1\% level.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

We report three comparisons. (A) The full system against the reactive baseline measures the value of the
whole architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:primary}, Table~\ref{tab:primary}). Of its 21 tests, 15 are significant
after Holm correction: 13 favour the full system and 2, both in the 4-robot swap, favour the baseline.
(B) Prediction with timing against prediction alone isolates the commander (Section~\ref{sec:which}). (C) The
full system against two controls that hold each change for the same window but release it after a random
wait or at the map's worst phase isolates the phase choice itself (Section~\ref{sec:phase-or-wait}).

\subsection{Which component does the work}\label{sec:which}
Prediction alone does not help (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}). Against the baseline it changes urgent events by
$+6\%$ in the 8-robot swap and $+3\%$ in the pillar field, and raises 99th-percentile tilt by 20\% in the
4-robot swap. Adding the commander, which holds each non-urgent change and releases it at the predicted best
phase, produces most of the gain (Table~\ref{tab:timing}): against prediction alone it lowers urgent events
by 18--20\% in the 8-robot swap and the pillar field and 99th-percentile tilt by 12--23\% in all four
scenarios. The gait-aware limiter adds further tilt reduction at the gate ($-15\%$ instead of $-12\%$ against
the baseline) but almost none elsewhere. Queueing matters in obstructed formations, where it doubles the
reduction in urgent events (pillars $-30\%$ instead of $-18\%$, gate $-34\%$ instead of $-14\%$) and widens
the closest distance by 7--8\%. The commander combines a bounded hold with a choice of phase;
Section~\ref{sec:phase-or-wait} separates the two.

\subsection{Phase choice or waiting}\label{sec:phase-or-wait}
The commander's benefit could come from releasing at the best phase or merely from holding the change for a
fraction of a gait period. The two controls hold for the same mean time (0.08--0.09\,s) and differ only in
when they release (Fig.~\ref{fig:controls}, Table~\ref{tab:controls}). Urgent events and closest distance do
not depend on the release phase: none of the 16 tests on these metrics is significant, and the random-wait
control alone reduces urgent events against the baseline by 27\% in the pillar field and 30\% at the gate.
The reduction in urgent events therefore comes from prediction and the bounded hold, not from the phase.
Tilt does depend on it, modestly and with the expected ordering: in every scenario the map's best phase gives
the lowest 99th-percentile tilt and its worst phase the highest. After Holm correction the best phase beats
the random wait in the 4-robot swap ($-11\%$) and the worst phase in the 4-robot swap and the pillar field
($-21\%$, $-8\%$). Pooled over the 80 seed pairs (an exploratory analysis), the best phase lowers p99 tilt by
5.9\% against the random wait ($p < 10^{-4}$) and the random wait by 4.4\% against the worst phase
($p = 0.0005$). In the crowded scenarios the phase choice accounts for 11--28\% of the full system's
reduction in p99 tilt.

\subsection{The capability map inside the swarm}\label{sec:map-context}
In the replay experiment (Section~\ref{sec:replay-setup}), 2\% of the 488 events met the conditions under which
the map was measured, and 92\% followed the previous command change by less than one gait period. In this
context the map's absolute predictions do not hold (Fig.~\ref{fig:mapctx}a): it under-predicts peak tilt by
$1.8^\circ$ on average (mean absolute error $2.1^\circ$, $R^2 = -0.99$, no better than the phase-blind map),
against $R^2 = 0.98$ for isolated steps. Its ranking of phases survives in part. The median rank correlation
between predicted cost and measured tilt over the nine release times is $+0.27$ (positive in 61\% of events),
and the phase the map chooses has $0.21^\circ$ less tilt than a random release (95\% CI $0.15$--$0.27^\circ$)
and $0.37^\circ$ less than its worst phase, which is 35\% of the reduction an oracle choosing the best of the
nine phases would achieve. The value of the map's choice falls with distance from the calibration conditions
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mapctx}b): it is $0.95^\circ$ when the previous change was at least one period ago and
$0.97^\circ$ for the largest changes (map size $\geq 0.5$), but $0.14^\circ$ during rapid command sequences
and $0.08^\circ$ above 0.4\,m/s. No replayed release led to a fall or to a tilt above $15^\circ$, so fall-risk
prediction could not be tested in context.

\subsection{Scaling}
The benefit holds as the team grows. With 16 robots in a swap the full system cuts urgent
events by 28\% and tilt by 24\%; with 32 robots, by 22\% and 19\%; with 16 robots in a formation through
pillars, by 39\% and 22\%. The gain in closest distance shrinks at 32 robots ($+2\%$, not significant), where
the swarm is near its packing limit.

\subsection{Cost}
Planning ahead and holding commands cost time and, in sparse traffic, safety margin. In the 4-robot swap the
full system is 6.8\% slower, has twice the urgent events of the baseline (5.2 against 10.9 per robot-minute)
and a 2\% smaller closest distance. In the 8-robot swap it is 2.7\% slower; at the gate the completion time is
unchanged. The random-wait control has the same cost (completion times within 0.6\% of the full system, not
significant), so the cost belongs to the hold, not to the phase choice.

\subsection{Lookahead}
Looking further ahead lets more changes be planned (Fig.~\ref{fig:lookahead}): the share
of manoeuvres that are urgent falls from 56\% (baseline) to 43\%, 41\%, 25\% and 22\% for $L$ = 0.18, 0.36,
0.54 and 0.72\,s in the 8-robot swap. But the number of command changes per robot-minute doubles from 54 to
112, because predictions made further ahead are revised more often. Tilt is reduced about equally at every
$L$. One gait period (0.36\,s) is the setting we use; 0.54\,s is slightly better at the gate but costs much
more command churn in swaps.

\subsection{Obstacle course}
For the team of six, the full system lowered 99th-percentile tilt from
$7.95^\circ$ to $6.68^\circ$ ($-16\%$), the worst tilt over 20 runs from $18.3^\circ$ to $13.7^\circ$, urgent
events by 14\% and robot--robot contacts from 0.25 to 0.05 per run, at the same completion time (129\,s).
Fig.~\ref{fig:course} shows one seed. One of its 20 runs did not finish in time; the baseline had one fall. For
a single robot the three configurations are nearly identical (tilt $4.7$--$4.9^\circ$), and prediction raised
urgent events (7.4 to 10.4 per minute), consistent with the 4-robot swap.

\subsection{Safety totals}\label{sec:safety-totals}
Across all 230 full-system runs in the final configuration there were no falls; the baseline fell twice in
170 runs (once in the 16-robot pillar field and once on the course). The full system had 16 robot--robot and
1 robot--obstacle contact onsets in those runs, against 28 and 4 for the baseline: contacts are rarer but
not eliminated (Section~\ref{sec:safety-scope}). Runs that did not finish in time were rare in every
configuration: one for the full system (course), one for queueing only, two for prediction only and one for
the worst-phase control.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{When it helps}
Planning ahead and holding commands pay off when a robot has many manoeuvres to make and enough warning to
plan them. Crowds and clutter provide both; sparse traffic does not. In the 4-robot swap the few manoeuvres are
mostly avoidable by acting slightly earlier, and any hold only delays them, which shows up as more urgent
events later; the random-wait control shows the same cost, so it belongs to the hold rather than to the phase
choice. A practical system should enable the hold only above a traffic-density threshold; we have not tested
such a switch.

\subsection{What the phase adds}
The controls separate two effects that the commander combines. Holding a non-urgent change for a fraction of a
gait period, with ORCA planning one period ahead, produces the reduction in urgent events and most of the
reduction in tilt; we have not isolated why the hold helps. Releasing at the phase the map predicts is safest
adds a smaller, consistent reduction in tilt (about 4--6\%), and releasing at the worst phase costs about as
much, so the phase matters to body stability but not to how often robots must swerve. The replay explains why
the phase effect is small: the map was measured on isolated steps from settled walking, and its ranking holds
well only for events that resemble those steps, which are a minority in a swarm. A map measured on command
sequences, with the time since the previous change as an additional input, is the natural next step.

\subsection{Robustness fixes and a rejected one}
The shared safety layer was not designed in advance. Each part
was added after a failure found by the experiments: robots falling after combined sideways walking and fast
turning (turn gate), robots pinned against a wall between them and their slot (escape), and robots grazing
pillars (static margin). Because the layer is identical in every configuration, it does not favour the full
system. We also tried a stronger escape rule, which detects a stall by lack of progress instead of low speed
and gives late robots right of way. It removed every fall, but runs that did not finish in time rose from 1 to
16 of 480 in the main suite, because the rule also fired during normal avoidance and sent robots on long
detours. We report it as a negative result; the code keeps it as an option.

\subsection{Limitations}
(i) All results are in simulation, with one robot model and one trot controller; the capability map must be
re-measured for another controller, and the effect size on hardware is unknown. (ii) The capability map is
measured on single command changes in settled walking; inside the swarm its absolute predictions do not hold
and its phase ranking holds only in part (Section~\ref{sec:map-context}). (iii) Collision avoidance is
validated empirically, not guaranteed: the prediction ignores the robot's own tracking error and contacts
still occur (Section~\ref{sec:safety-scope}). (iv) Robots share positions and velocities perfectly, without
noise or delay. (v) The scaling suite has only 10 seeds, which limits the attainable significance, and the
pooled analysis of the timing controls is exploratory. (vi) Completion time rises by up to 7\% in sparse
scenarios. (vii) Falls were too rare to test the map's fall-risk predictions in context.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A quadruped's tolerance of a velocity change depends on where in the gait cycle the change arrives. A swarm
layer that plans ORCA one gait period ahead, holds each non-urgent change for at most half a period and
releases it at the predicted best phase cut urgent avoidance events by 22--39\% and 99th-percentile tilt by
19--24\% against a reactive baseline in crowded and obstructed scenarios with 6, 9, 16 and 32 simulated
Go1 robots,
with no falls in 230 runs against two in 170 for the baseline. Controls show that planning ahead and the
bounded hold account for the reduction in urgent events, and that the phase choice adds a smaller, consistent
reduction in tilt. A capability map measured on isolated steps keeps only part of its phase ranking in the rapid
command sequences of a swarm. Next steps are a map measured on command sequences, hardware experiments on Go1
robots, a second low-level controller (a learned policy), sensing noise and delay, and an automatic switch that
disables the hold in sparse traffic.
